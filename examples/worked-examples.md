# Worked examples

All people, observations, thresholds, and budgets below are fictional. Use them to see how the decision responds to different inputs. Set your own criteria before collecting data.

## A guided review from one idea

> I have an idea for an app that helps independent bookshops manage special orders. Is it a good idea? What would I need to make it work?

**Start with TEST.** One possible problem is that staff lose track of orders, but the idea alone leaves that open. Start with “What made you think special orders are hard for bookshops to manage?” If the builder can reach a shop, ask someone to show how they handled their most recent order. If access is limited, look for a firsthand account of the workflow and use it to plan a conversation. The useful next evidence is a specific delay, mistake, or repeated workaround.

Suppose the builder later brings examples from two shops. Both track orders reliably, but staff forget to tell customers when an order is ready. The revised problem is customer notification at pickup, with the records supporting it at those shops. Compare adding a reminder to the existing workflow with an app. If a shop is willing to try it, the next **TEST** could be to use a simple reminder on a few live orders and record whether staff send updates on time. If the shops already send timely updates but customers miss them, investigate the delivery or wording of those updates before proposing another reminder.

## Find a problem before proposing a product

A shop owner is considering some kind of repair-shop app but can spend only one morning exploring it. They recall one delayed repair while waiting for a part, one afternoon of interrupted work answering status calls, and one awkward appointment reschedule. Each is a candidate problem. The owner can trace several recent status calls using a redacted shop log, so that is the most accessible starting point.

**TEST** the hypothesis that customers call about status because expected update times are unclear. In one morning, review a small, bounded set of recent jobs with the shop's permission. Compare jobs that received a proactive update with jobs that did not, and look for reasons other than uncertainty. Use the observations to decide whether to interview customers about the last time they called. A single shop's records support a decision about that shop. Expansion to other shops needs independent evidence.

## TEST · Estimate reminders for specialist installers

**Commitment.** Spend one week investigating. The proposed product can wait.

**Problem hypothesis.** Small window-installation firms with no dedicated salesperson lose track of estimates when site visits bunch together. They currently rely on their inbox, and follow-ups may be delayed.

**Evidence and countercase.** One owner describes a specific delayed follow-up (C2, one firm). A different owner already uses calendar tasks and says follow-up works well (C2, possible boundary case). Three friends like the idea (C1, outside the segment). The actual cost and frequency remain unknown.

**Next action.** The builder can spend four hours and no money, so ask five qualifying firms about their most recent estimate week. Seek redacted artifacts when offered. Suppose the criteria chosen in advance are as follows.

- **Pass.** At least three of five completed interviews describe a recent delay with an observable consequence, and at least two supply separate supporting artifacts with permission. Consider a narrow manual reminder test, subject to capacity.
- **Fail.** All five complete the interview, at most one describes such a delay, and clear well-used alternatives explain the other cases. Close this segment and framing.
- **Inconclusive.** Fewer than five eligible conversations, ambiguous episodes, or results between the two thresholds. Review the framing and recruitment route before allocating another block.

## GO · A bounded pilot with a budget owner

Separate observation notes show three independent firms manually following up after missed reminders (C3 for workflow). Two owners paid for a small manual trial and used it on real estimates (C4 for this offer and sample). A third firm was already satisfied with its calendar (counterexample). The builder has six hours next week and will defer one optional side project.

**GO** for one more week with the two pilot customers, capped at six hours and no new infrastructure. Compare completed follow-ups and delivery effort with the pilot's original targets at the end of the week. The purchase and use support this bounded step. Repeatable acquisition and profitability remain open.

## HOLD · Real expense, unavailable access

Two workshop managers show independent redacted records of missed handovers (C3 for those sites). A meaningful trial requires staff access the buyer cannot authorize until next quarter. The builder has a fixed client deadline in the meantime.

**HOLD** until the buyer can grant supervised access and the client deadline clears. Set a review for the first week of next quarter. Use a shorter review slot only if both conditions change.

## STOP · An adequate alternative in the chosen segment

The proposal is a paid add-on that sends reminders for a workflow inside an existing platform. In a fictional test with six matching teams, all six demonstrate a built-in reminder they already use and none accepts the clearly priced pilot on real terms. A completed pilot booking had been the advance criterion for continuing. One team struggles with a related approval bottleneck, which the reminder would not address.

**STOP** the reminder add-on for this segment. Record the approval bottleneck as a separate hypothesis. Reopen the reminder direction only if concrete evidence identifies a reachable segment with unmet needs despite the built-in feature.

## TEST · Paid demand is one part of the case

Two independent repair shops describe reconstruction work after missed handoffs. Their managers paid for a manual evening summary and used it during live shifts (C4 for those offers). Producing it required 50 minutes a night with extra unpaid help (C3 for that setup). The managers paid enough to cover only 20 minutes of the builder's time per summary. Both asked for reliability during the late shift.

**TEST** whether a narrower delivery process can keep the promised result within the existing terms. Compare three more real handoffs with the manual baseline, recording time, errors, help, and delivery delays. A pass requires meeting the delivery deadline every night, preserving the key information both managers use, and getting within the 20-minute cost limit. More than one missed deadline or a clear overrun on each comparable night defeats the current process at this price. A shift without normal exposure or bad time records makes the result inconclusive. The builder caps the comparison at one week. A pass supports another bounded pilot. It leaves repeatable acquisition open.

## TEST · A creative project has more than one route

A builder wants to make a personal audio tour so a friend who is new to their neighborhood can recognize three landmarks on a walk. They feel stuck designing a map interface. They can finish one small artifact this evening.

**TEST** whether a single spoken route guides the walk. Sketch a paper route, record a short audio sample, and choose the fastest option that preserves the experience the builder wants. Ask the friend to follow it and note where they hesitate. If they can find the landmarks while keeping their phone in their pocket, expand the audio route. If they repeatedly lose their bearings, test landmark descriptions or a small map next. Measure the experience the builder cares about.

## TEST · A small routine gets the work back in motion

A builder planned to interview three venue managers. They have used their Friday blocks to redesign slides and start a second idea. An existing peer check-in is the one time they reliably prepare.

**TEST** a simpler routine for the next two check-ins. After writing the usual agenda, open the last research note and prepare one neutral question about a recent venue handover. Use the protected block to book or complete one authorized conversation. Save what was observed and the decision it affects. If contact remains hard to reach after two attempts, change the recruitment route. If a new idea appears during the block, save it for the scheduled review. A kept time block measures follow-through, and the interview supplies evidence about the work.

## TEST · Share a small artifact and observe who acts

A builder wants to learn whether venue managers need a better handover. A broad audience liked a polished illustration, but nobody described a recent handover. The builder has permission to post a de-identified example and can reach a relevant peer community.

**TEST** with one useful before-and-after handover sketch. Ask managers who completed a handover this month what step created the most waiting. Record the number of eligible respondents who describe a specific case or agree to show a redacted example. Use that to find interview candidates. Evaluate real repeated use separately if the builder later offers a pilot.

## TEST · Prove the process survives a handoff

Four venue managers paid for a report and acted on it. The original builder can finish each report in 25 minutes. Other teammates have to ask them about an undocumented exception and take twice as long.

**TEST** one handoff with three comparable reports. Freeze the time allowance and one useful result for customers. Document the input, exception rule, owner, and quality check. Have a teammate run the process with the permissions they would normally have, and record timing, mistakes, and rescue calls. If the teammate meets the agreed standard without rescue, repeat under normal conditions before expanding. If not, change the exception rule or narrow the promise. Paid demand and independent delivery are separate claims.

## TEST · Orders contribute, and capacity still falls short

A builder charges 120 per order. Comparable orders cost 60 each to acquire, deliver, and support, including the builder's time. An ongoing service would add 600 a month in fixed costs. At the observed contribution of 60 per order, it takes 10 orders a month to cover those costs. The builder can currently deliver eight, which would contribute 480.

**TEST** a narrower scope before committing to the fixed costs. Pick one result customers already acted on and keep the other terms clear. Measure time, quality and contribution on a small comparable batch. In parallel, count the eligible people reached, paying orders, and repeat use when a second need occurs. Advance only if both the channel and delivery can plausibly support the revised target. A full schedule at eight orders still leaves a gap of 120 per month under the current assumptions.
