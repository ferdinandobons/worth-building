# Test whether you can deliver it sustainably

Use when the buyer or user has acted, the project is ready for a bounded release, or an operation keeps depending on one person. Choose the risk closest to changing the next commitment.

## Define the result and a commitment you can afford

Write one sentence about what this project should make possible in the builder's life or work. Translate it into a delivery standard and constraints the builder can check, such as capacity, price, error rate, turnaround, support hours, and what happens on a day off. Use rough ranges for unknown costs. In a business, check whether the required number of orders can both be reached and served with existing resources. For a hobby or internal project, use the appropriate outcome, time, and upkeep.

For a rough commercial model, subtract the costs that vary with an order from its net revenue. Include acquisition, fees, delivery, support, and rework where applicable. Value the builder's delivery time and count it once. The remainder contributes to fixed costs. When that remainder is positive, divide the fixed costs for a period by the contribution per order and round up to estimate the orders needed to cover them. A zero or negative contribution cannot cover positive fixed costs by adding volume at the same price and cost. Compare the required orders with what the channel can plausibly bring in and what the team can actually deliver. Test the most sensitive estimate in a small real batch.

List the assumptions behind the plan. Trace a real case from how someone finds the offer, to the first useful result, to the next reason to return or pay. Locate the bottleneck with the largest consequence for this step. Measure it before spending time refining a later stage. Price, traffic, and margins in a rough model are explicit assumptions until observed.

Use a period that fits the work. Count eligible people reached, who received an offer, who got the first useful result, and who paid or returned at the next real opportunity. Track denominators and keep those without a second opportunity visible. Ask where the largest meaningful loss occurs. Choose one channel, onboarding, retention, or capacity check for that constraint. Revisit it after a comparable period before committing to more volume.

Set a fixed time and spending allowance. Shape a smaller scope that preserves one complete, useful result. Cut optional features, steps, approvals, and polish that do not affect the outcome. Name the minimum quality, safety, or reliability needed before real use. Treat the plan as a forecast to revise when you learn. Use a manual delivery or a small release where that gives faster evidence.

## Stage the operation around real work

Early on, observe the work while doing it. As demand repeats, test whether delivery still works when volume rises or a person changes. Avoid solving the future staffing plan before you understand a representative case.

1. **Assign responsibilities.** Identify who decides the promise and limits, who maintains the way the work happens, and who performs it. The same person can hold all three responsibilities. Name the output and authority for each.
2. **Measure the current process.** Follow a real job from intake through completion. Record inputs, time by stage, waiting, handoffs, rework, cost, customer outcome, and where personal judgment or special access mattered.
3. **Improve one bottleneck.** Change one step, such as the intake checklist or the format of a handoff. Keep comparable jobs, record the same measures, and check whether time saved created errors or shifted effort elsewhere.
4. **Write down the smallest stable procedure.** Capture the trigger, necessary input, accountable owner, steps, quality check, exception path, and result. Save a representative example. Use a short checklist or template that can be run during real work.
5. **Test a handoff.** Have another eligible person, or the same person after a meaningful gap, run a comparable case using only the available instructions and permissions. Count their questions, errors, completion time, and any rescue by the original builder. Revise the procedure and run it again if it remains promising.
6. **Choose what to repeat.** Keep the change when it improves the outcome within the agreed limit. Revisit the scope, price, staffing, or promise when it cannot. Add automation, tools, or people when the evidence shows a recurring constraint they can remove.

For a release, watch the first actual use and support effort. Prefer smaller reversible changes when the team can observe them. Handle feedback according to the underlying job and repeated evidence. Test whether a requested feature would resolve the bottleneck before adding it. If coordination is consuming the time needed to deliver, use a short written decision or a working example and agree who needs to respond.

Name the trade-off when a request would change what the product promises. If a release misses the promise, explain the effect and the next step plainly to the people affected, with authorization. Check recurring work before adding a role. A clear recurring responsibility and a testable result give a basis for delegation or hiring.

Use the [experiment guide](experiments.md) to choose an operating check and the [evidence rubric](evidence.md) to scope what a successful handoff or sustainable delivery proves. A profitable order and a repeatable business require different evidence.
