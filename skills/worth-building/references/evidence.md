# Evidence rubric

Rate each observation against **one named claim and population**. Record direction as supports, challenges, or mixed. The level measures the directness of the evidence for that claim. A C3 observation about a workaround can be C0 for willingness to pay for a proposed product.

| Level | What qualifies | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **C0 · Unknown** | An assumption, forecast, AI-generated example, unavailable source, or missing data | Choose a question to investigate. |
| **C1 · Indirect** | Compliments, future intent, anonymous comments with thin context, search interest, or a competitor's marketing claim | Find vocabulary and people to speak with. A competitor's page may directly establish its advertised price, with a date and region. |
| **C2 · Firsthand account** | An identifiable-in-context account of a recent, specific episode from someone in the segment, with trigger, actions, and consequences. A substantiated complaint can qualify. | Learn how the problem occurs and compare independent accounts. |
| **C3 · Observed behavior** | A relevant work artifact, workflow observation, usage record, or documented behavior with enough context to interpret it | Check frequency, cost, switching, or use in the observed setting. |
| **C4 · Costly action** | A completed action that costs the relevant person meaningful time, money, effort, or reputation. Examples include actually paying, using a pilot in real work, or returning unprompted for a second decision. | Evaluate commitment to the exact offer or behavior observed. Record terms, incentives, and follow-through. |

Treat statements of future action according to what actually happened. A booked pilot is evidence of scheduling. Attendance, active use, repeated use, and payment each support different claims.

## Check a confident claim

Write the claim in a form a specific observation could challenge. “Everyone would pay” leaves demand open. Ask which buyers had the problem recently, what they did, and what they accepted on clear terms. If the user describes two payments in detail, record them as reported payments in that scope and ask for supporting records when the next commitment warrants it. An uninspected record leaves more uncertainty than a record you have checked. Treat the user as a possible firsthand source and keep the level tied to what they can describe or show.

Ask what would tell you that the current explanation is wrong. A manager may praise a reminder while already using a tool that solves the problem. Prefer a comparison that could expose a sufficient alternative, show the issue is rare, or reveal that the real buyer values a different outcome. Record what would count against the claim before testing it.

## Claims to keep separate

- **Problem and consequence.** Who encounters it, under what trigger, how frequently within a relevant period, and with what measurable effect? Rare incidents may still matter if the consequence is severe.
- **Current behavior.** What do people already do or pay to get the job done? How well does that work? Include manual work, delegation, competing priorities, and doing nothing.
- **Adoption and value.** Will the user switch, invest effort, and return? If the business depends on payment, who controls the budget and what have they committed on real terms?
- **Delivery, reach, and sustainability.** Can the builder reach the segment and deliver the outcome within realistic costs and constraints? What maintenance, operational burden, or permissions matter next?

## Assess confidence

- **Low.** Decisive evidence is missing, indirect, narrow, stale, dependent on one source, or materially conflicted.
- **Medium.** Multiple independent, in-scope accounts or a direct observation support the decisive claim. Explain the unresolved selection, coverage, or generalizability limits.
- **High.** Relevant direct evidence converges across independent observations and survives a credible attempt to disconfirm it. Specify exactly where it applies. A small pilot can give high confidence in an event that happened and low confidence in how often it would happen elsewhere.

Check relevance, recency, independence, incentives, sample selection, and negative cases. Duplicate posts, several people reporting the same incident, and stories all relayed by one intermediary may share provenance. For quantitative claims, capture the denominator, sampling frame, period, and missing cases. A convenience sample can guide a pilot. Its counts do not establish market prevalence.

Look at how much the observed action cost the participant and what else could explain it. A friendly paid trial funded by an unrelated budget may establish payment while telling you little about demand from the target buyer. A workflow recording can establish current effort while leaving preferred replacements open. Keep confidence in a fact separate from confidence in its implication.

Record each entry with a stable ID, claim, direction, observation, evidence level, source or private participant ID, date, population, and caveat. Keep a separate note for your interpretation. Preserve contradictory and inconclusive results. Avoid adding up evidence levels or converting confidence into a probability of success.

If there is no observed signal, say which evidence was sought and how exposed the test was. No replies after ten unconfirmed deliveries and ten relevant users who tried an offer and passed are different observations.
