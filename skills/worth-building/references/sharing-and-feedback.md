# Share work to learn and find the right people

Use when the builder needs to reach eligible participants, show a working idea, or learn from feedback. Choose the smallest shareable artifact that helps someone and can answer one question. Useful formats include a redacted workflow sketch, a short before-and-after demo, an explanation of a learned technique, or a note about an experiment and what changed.

## Pick a purpose, an audience, and one next action

1. **Name who should see it.** Choose people who recently did the work, evaluated an alternative, or can make the decision you care about. Start where those people already exchange useful information. Participate, help and listen. A broad audience can be useful for teaching or visibility. It gives weaker context for a narrow demand claim.
2. **Show something specific.** Share a small piece of work in progress or a completed useful result. Say who it helps, what the artifact shows today, what stage it is at, and what remains uncertain. Explain a useful technique in enough detail that someone can try it.
3. **Ask for one real response.** Request a concrete past episode, a correction, a redacted comparison, a conversation, or an agreed trial depending on the decision. For example, “If you closed a delayed shift handoff this month, what step took the longest?” For trial invitations, make expectations and terms clear.
4. **Choose what is safe to share.** Get authorization before publishing or contacting people. Share only work the user is entitled to share. Use permission, attribution when needed, and redaction for other people's work and information. Keep raw interview notes, internal plans, and identifying customer details private.
5. **Keep useful pieces findable.** Use clear titles and maintain a durable home for a few useful updates with dates and changes. Choose a cadence supported by real work, adapting length and channel to the audience. A private demo or small peer group may be the right venue.

If helpful, draft in this order. **Situation** [who and when]. **Artifact** [what they can see or reuse]. **Learning** [what changed and why]. **Invitation** [one action or question]. Keep claims within the evidence and leave the user in control of sending or publishing.

## Interpret feedback without losing the decision

Track who could see the update, who fits the target situation, and what each eligible person actually did. Keep impressions, likes, meaningful replies, completed conversations, usable contributions, and trial behavior distinct. Praise can signal interest in the post. It can also come from people who would never face the problem. Follow specific objections with a question about the person's experience. Take a break or narrow the channel when feedback is noisy or costly.

Bring repeated in-scope observations back into the [evidence rubric](evidence.md). Surface the strongest relevant criticism alongside the strongest supporting observation. Treat a suggestion as a clue to the job before adding it to scope. Track negative cases, missing exposure, and replies from people outside the target. A small update can bring attention. Adoption or payment still needs its own test.

After a release or completed experiment, share a useful result, describe a changed assumption, or close the loop with participants who asked for an update. Review whether sharing led to the action you sought before committing to more content.
