# Find the right problem

## Start where you can see the work

Choose a role and a recurring or high-stakes situation you can reach. A good starting setting has a concrete trigger, for example a repair handoff, an unexpected expense, or the first shift after a delivery fails. List the people involved and the artifacts the work leaves behind. If the idea is for the user's own use, observe their own workflow and keep the claim personal until evidence reaches further.

Collect specific moments. Look for wait time, repeated copying, improvised checklists, costly human help, work people abandon, errors they catch at the last minute, and tasks they avoid altogether. Also seek a comparable occasion when the job went smoothly. The difference can expose a boundary condition that is more useful than another complaint.

The builder's own frustration can be a useful lead. Check whether other people have the same trigger, consequences, and options before treating it as a shared need. For people who never use the category at all, ask what they were trying to achieve, what kept them out, and whether they tried something else to make progress.

Write one line per episode with the trigger, what the person tried to achieve, the steps they took, the outcome, and the direct consequence. A request for a feature is a prompt to investigate. Ask when its absence mattered and what the person did at that point.

## Trace the decision to change

For each relevant episode, follow the timeline from the first sign of trouble through the current outcome. Note the first moment the old approach became unacceptable, when they started looking, what options entered the picture, who had to agree, and what they did after choosing. Ask what made the new option attractive and what they feared losing. Identify the habit, trust, training, files, contracts, access, or social obligation that kept the old approach in place.

Include people who stayed with the existing method and people who never started. Someone who accepts a delay may be making a reasonable trade. Someone who never tries may have a constraint that a simpler product could remove. Look for an observation that would tell these stories apart.

Ask what success meant in that episode. Look for practical results, feelings, and effects on how someone is seen by others when the story supports them. An account manager might need to send an accurate update, feel sure it went out, and avoid appearing unreliable. Each part suggests a different way of testing whether the experience improved.

## Compare candidates before choosing a solution

Name one outcome that matters and sketch the possible routes to it.

1. **Outcome.** What observable change would make a difference, and for whom? Connect it to the user or business result at stake. For example, a customer receiving a dependable first update is a more immediate outcome to test than higher annual revenue.
2. **Candidate problems.** Group episodes by the progress people struggled to make. Split broad labels into concrete problems with different triggers or causes. Keep the evidence IDs with each candidate.
3. **Compare.** Consider consequence, frequency in a meaningful period, what people already spend, whether the current response works, who has authority, and how readily the next uncertainty can be checked. Compare needs of similar scope, such as two triggers within a handoff, before comparing designs. Flag unknowns. Favor a reachable problem with a consequential and demonstrably unmet need. Keep rare but severe problems visible.
4. **Responses.** Once a problem warrants attention, compare at least two plausible ways to improve the outcome when alternatives are available. Include refining the current process, providing a manual service, or keeping the status quo when relevant. Walk through how a real person would discover, start, and get value from each response.
5. **Assumptions.** Mark which claim about demand, access, feasibility, economics, or adoption differs across responses. Test the claim that changes the choice. If new evidence weakens the problem, reopen the problem comparison.

A useful problem statement can be disproved. “Teams need a dashboard” leaves the decision vague. “When the evening shift arrives without a handoff, dispatchers call technicians to reconstruct unresolved jobs, delaying reassignment” tells you whose shift, what to observe, and where an existing solution may already suffice.

## Keep a short learning loop

After each observation or interview, update the record while the details are fresh. While the project is active, use a [learning rhythm](learning-rhythm.md) that creates another chance to learn before the next major commitment. At each review, record the decision that changed, the question that is still open, and the one next observation that could change it.
