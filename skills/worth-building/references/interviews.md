# Discovery interviews

## Plan one useful conversation

- Pick two or three questions the current decision depends on. Include one that could overturn your favorite explanation. Note who could answer each question from direct experience.
- Recruit for a concrete event and role. A recent search, switch, escalation, workaround, or decision to do nothing is a better screen than general interest. Include people who chose differently when you can.
- Recruit where the work happens or through specific referrals. Use more than one route if practical. Track recruitment route and who the screen excludes. A circle of enthusiastic friends can rehearse a script. It gives little evidence about the target audience unless the friends qualify on their own.
- Use a neutral invitation about the situation and how people deal with it. Keep participation voluntary. Get authorization before outreach, explain the note-taking purpose, get permission to record or view an artifact, and keep only necessary, redacted details. If two team members join, agree who leads and who takes notes.

For an early batch, use a shared screen so the stories are comparable and leave room for a few people who handled the situation differently. If the first conversations reveal a different trigger, improve the next screen. Record the change before mixing the batches. Short conversations in context can be useful when they yield a specific episode.

## Follow the episode

Open with a recent event the participant can remember. Leave the pitch out of problem discovery. Follow their sequence, allow pauses, and use short questions. Choose the prompts that fit.

1. “Take me back to the most recent time [trigger] happened. What were you trying to get done?”
2. “What did you do first? What happened after that?”
3. “When did your usual approach become difficult? What happened when you put it off or skipped a step?”
4. “What did this instance cost you or the team? Could you show me how you work that out?”
5. “When did you first look for another way? What options came up, and who helped you choose?”
6. “What did the old way do well? What made a change feel risky or inconvenient?”
7. “What happened the first time you used the new approach? Did you keep using it?”
8. “Can you recall a case where this went smoothly? What was different?”

If the participant struggles to remember an event, offer a neutral anchor such as the last working week, invoice, or shift. Record uncertainty if the event remains vague. At the end, ask what you missed and, if appropriate, who had a different experience.

## Recover from vague or leading answers

| What you hear | Follow-up |
| --- | --- |
| “It happens all the time.” | “Which instance stands out? What happened during the last full cycle?” |
| “I would definitely use that.” | “What did you use last time? When did you last try an alternative?” |
| “Just give it a dashboard.” | “What would you need to decide or do when you opened it? How did you make that decision last time?” |
| “That price is fine.” | “Who approved the last comparable purchase? How did that go?” If you're evaluating a real offer, bring the actual terms to the budget owner. |
| “Everything works fine.” | “Could you walk me through the last smooth case?” Keep it as a potential counterexample. |

Explore the problem and the offer in separate phases. Once you are explicitly testing an offer, ask for an appropriate next action with clear terms. Distinguish a promised introduction from an introduction made, a planned trial from a trial used in real work, and an agreement to pay from payment.

Ask who took the last relevant decision and what they actually had to approve. If somebody offers help, make the next step concrete, such as a completed introduction, a follow-up with an agenda, or a trial with one real task. Record what they agreed to and what happened separately. An unanswered follow-up leaves a different signal from an explicit refusal on clear terms.

## Debrief and repeat

Capture participant ID, role, date, recruitment route, trigger, actions, old approach, cost, outcome, and any work shown or commitment completed. Record the participant's account and your interpretation separately. Quote exact words only when recorded accurately. Mark paraphrases. Assign evidence levels against specific claims.

After each session, ask what surprised you, what challenged the hypothesis, and what remains unknown. Compare episodes from independent sources within the same situation. Seek someone who had a different result. Repeat while a fresh observation can still shape the next decision.
