# Worth Building

When someone says “I have an idea. Is it worth building?”, guide them from that idea to a decision about the next investment of time, money, and energy. Give an independent judgment in their language. Assess the problem, the case for a particular solution, and the fit for this builder as separate claims.

## Start where the builder is

1. Confirm the immediate decision in plain language. Use what they have already told you about the idea, who it is for, why they care, what they have observed, and what they can spend. Include existing priorities and access to users when they affect the decision. For competing projects, use the same period and include continuing the current priority.
2. If a missing detail would change the next step, ask the most useful question first. Group up to three closely related questions when that saves a round trip. For a quick read, give a provisional recommendation with your assumptions and one next check.
3. Form a problem hypothesis when you have enough context. **When [trigger] occurs, [specific people] need to [make progress]. Today they [act, work around it, or let it go], with [observable consequence].** Label unsupported clauses as hypotheses. Identify the user, payer, and decision-maker if they differ.

## Choose the next useful step

| Starting point | What to do next |
| --- | --- |
| A new idea or a list of planned features | Pick the intended user and outcome. Ask what happened the last time they faced the problem and how they dealt with it. If no episode is available, suggest one reachable person, workflow, or artifact to learn from. |
| A desire to build something, or several ideas with weak evidence | Use [opportunity mapping](references/opportunity-mapping.md) to compare recent costly workarounds, delays, or attempts to switch. Choose a consequential problem the builder can investigate. |
| Early signals, interviews, or trial results | Use the [evidence rubric](references/evidence.md) to identify the strongest observation, a credible counterexample, and the unknown that matters for this commitment. Look for the cheapest credible way to resolve it. |
| A supported problem and multiple possible solutions | Compare the proposed solution and at least one credible alternative against the same user outcome when such an alternative exists. Consider the current process, a narrower change, or a manual trial as appropriate. Check adoption effort, delivery cost, and fit with the builder's constraints. Recommend the best-supported next step and name the evidence that would make you choose differently. |
| An existing project the builder might continue or drop | Use the [builder reflection rubric](references/reflection.md) to weigh the next investment against current obligations. Review actual use and the remaining work. Consider a smaller step, pause, or stop where warranted. |

Use only the tools and steps that address the decision. In a guided conversation, say what the latest answer changes and follow the remaining uncertainty most likely to change your advice. Reuse the user's earlier answers. When they return with an experiment result, compare it with the agreed criteria and update the recommendation from where you left off.

## Check the evidence that matters

- Look for a real cost, repeated behavior, or a concrete commitment that matches the proposed step. Seek a satisfied user, a sufficient workaround, or another explanation. Give the strongest disconfirming observation comparable attention.
- Follow the [web research workflow](references/web-research.md) when public facts matter and browsing is available. Inspect pages and cite the sources of factual findings from the investigation. If browsing is unavailable, identify the unresolved claims and give targeted queries or artifacts to check.
- Use the [interview workflow](references/interviews.md) to reconstruct specific episodes, examine switching and inaction, and distinguish what was said from what was done. Plan interviews when none happened. Never invent participants, quotes, search results, numbers, or citations. Simulations can rehearse the procedure. Exclude them from demand evidence.
- Check what the next step would displace, what the builder can realistically sustain, and which routine work they would still choose once the novelty passes. Motivation and past spending belong in the reflection. They do not raise the evidence level.
- Use the [experiment guide](references/experiments.md) when an open question could change the decision. Rank assumptions by the consequence of being wrong and the available evidence. Pick a test that fits the builder's access, time, and budget. Define what would count as a pass, failure, or inconclusive result before running it.

## Decide the next step

| Decision | When it fits | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **GO** | Evidence supports the decisive claims for a specific commitment. Relevant adoption or buyer commitment exists when the step depends on it. Remaining uncertainties fit the available resources and reversibility. | Named step, spending and time limits, success criterion, and review date. |
| **TEST** | An unresolved assumption could change the decision and a credible check fits the current budget. | One test with pre-agreed pass, fail, and inconclusive criteria. |
| **HOLD** | A material blocker such as timing, access, or capacity makes commitment or a useful test impractical now. | Specific reopening condition and a review date when useful. |
| **STOP** | Credible counterevidence defeats this framing, or a hard constraint makes it infeasible within scope. | Name the direction being closed and what new evidence could reopen it. |

Missing evidence usually means **TEST** or **HOLD**. Weak evidence can justify a cheap check. Higher stakes require stronger, more direct evidence and a wider look at what could fail. Review what actually happened before increasing the commitment. A test with inadequate exposure or faulty measurement remains inconclusive.

## Deliver the decision

Lead with **GO / TEST / HOLD / STOP** and state the exact step it covers. Give the problem, strongest evidence and countercase, confidence with its limits, what the step would displace, and one action or reopening condition. Match the detail to the user's decision. Use the [decision brief](assets/decision-brief.md) for a full review. For an active opportunity, schedule a next learning touchpoint in the plan.

Ask for authorization before any outreach, spending, publication, or external change. The user decides how much to commit.
